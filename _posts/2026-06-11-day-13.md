After resolving yesterday's preprocessing bugs, I successfully implemented Experiments 4, 5, and 6 to rigorously test feature stability and cross-modal gating logic.
  In Experiment 4, I evaluated the Morphology Preservation Index (MPI) under label-changing clinical mutations (ST-elevation and QRS widening). The CFD-optimal branch achieved an MPI of 0.9800 compared to 0.7400 in the entangled Non-CFD space, producing a Morphology Preservation Gain (MPG) of +0.2400 (p = 2.85e-110).
  For Experiment 5, I built out the Trust Hierarchy Validation pipeline. When analyzing the 164 cross-modal disagreement cases where the parallel channels conflicted, I integrated the Level 2 Respiratory Tiebreaker rule. Initial tests yielded an SVR of 9.17%, which my mentor identified as too high for clinical deployment due to the risk of alarm fatigue. By shifting from a flat 90th percentile threshold to an independent, high-percentile cross-modal gated boundary (99.75th percentile), I successfully calibrated the Safe Violation Rate (SVR) down to an optimal range of 0.20%–0.50% while maintaining an Encoder Drift Correlation (EDC) of 0.7914, exceeding the required protocol threshold of 0.75.
  This calibrated system successfully recovered diagnostic accuracy on conflicting streams, improving from a baseline fallback accuracy of 46.30% to a Tiebreaker Resolution Accuracy (TRA) of 81.48%.
  Finally, I executed Experiment 6 to quantify the Multimodal Drift Advantage (MDA). The results demonstrated that as monitoring environments become more hostile, the multimodal advantage expands significantly—rising from a +7.30% accuracy gain at Level 1 (Agreement) to a +47.00% diagnostic advantage at Level 3 (Human-in-the-Loop), while maintaining resilience under out-of-distribution thermal sensor drift.

blockers: |

  Resolving this issue required redesigning the decision boundary into an independent, high-percentile cross-modal gate. This adjustment significantly reduced false alarms while preserving drift detection performance.

A false alarm rate of 9.17% may seem acceptable in some machine learning settings, but in a clinical environment it could contribute to alarm fatigue and reduce trust in the monitoring system.
  Learning how to mathematically tune statistical thresholds to reduce benign drift violations to a fraction of a percent, while preserving meaningful tracking correlation, was a valuable lesson in safety-critical system design.
  The results from the Multimodal Drift Advantage experiment were particularly encouraging. Observing the multimodal framework gain strength as disagreement and uncertainty increased reinforced the value of the trust hierarchy architecture. These findings provide strong evidence that the framework's greatest benefits emerge under challenging and conflicting monitoring conditions, which aligns closely with the central goals of my summer research project.
