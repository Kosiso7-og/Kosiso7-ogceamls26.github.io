what_i_learned: |
  Today began with our research lab session, where I continued working on the remaining experiments for my project. After several days of implementing the experimental pipeline, reviewing unexpected results, resolving coding errors, and checking that my labels, features, predictions, and train-test splits were properly aligned, I was able to successfully complete all of the experiments. Reaching this point felt rewarding because each experiment built on the results and methodology of the previous one.
  The experiments examined several aspects of reliable multimodal biosignal prediction, including complementary feature-domain selection, benign and non-benign drift classification, cross-modal agreement, stability violations, CFD contribution to reliability, and the comparison between multimodal and unimodal models. I also evaluated how the models responded to out-of-distribution noise. Completing the experiments helped me better understand that obtaining an output is only one part of research. It is equally important to verify that the data used by each model is correctly aligned, ensure that the evaluation metrics are calculated consistently, and interpret results honestly when they do not match the expected hypothesis.
  Before the lunch break, we attended a session with Dr. Crawford from the University of Alabama. I enjoyed listening to him discuss his work in brain-computer interfaces. It was interesting to learn about a field that connects neuroscience, computing, signal processing, and human interaction. His presentation showed how technology can interpret signals from the brain and potentially use them to support communication, control, rehabilitation, and other real-world applications. The session also helped me recognize the broader range of problems that can be studied using physiological signals and machine learning.
  After Dr. Crawford's session, each group in the cohort gave a brief overview of its research project, followed by a screening of the group's pitch video. I enjoyed watching the videos and seeing the different creative approaches the groups used to explain their work. The videos provided a quick and engaging way to understand the scope of everyone's project, even when the research topics were very different. We were also given the opportunity to vote for our top three pitch videos, and I selected the groups whose ideas, presentation styles, and creativity stood out to me.
  After lunch, I began documenting the outputs from my research experiments in a shared document for my teammates to review. I organized the major results, evaluation metrics, observations, and areas that may require further discussion. This step was important because completing the code does not automatically make the findings easy to understand. The results now need to be presented clearly so that my team can review the methodology, verify the interpretations, and decide what should be emphasized in our final report and presentation.

blockers: |
  Although I successfully completed all of the experiments today, I still encountered challenges while finalizing the pipeline. Some variables had been overwritten or were no longer aligned with the full dataset, particularly the label arrays, model predictions, and train-test indices used in later experiments. I had to carefully validate that the ECG, PPG, respiratory features, labels, and indices all referred to the same 5,000 samples before continuing.
  I also encountered an issue with the pandas installation in the notebook environment. The numerical calculations for one of the later experiments completed successfully, but the code failed while attempting to reshape the results for a figure. I worked around this by creating the visualization directly with Matplotlib instead of relying on the affected pandas pivot operation. This reminded me that an error occurring near the end of a code block does not necessarily mean the entire experiment failed. In this case, the model training, predictions, Multimodal Drift Advantage calculations, and out-of-distribution evaluation had already completed before the plotting error occurred.
  The next challenge is ensuring that the completed results are interpreted appropriately. Some experimental outcomes did not follow the expected pattern, and several models performed close to chance. Rather than presenting these results as evidence for conclusions they do not support, I need to clearly distinguish between successful implementation, partial support for a hypothesis, and findings that were statistically insignificant or contrary to expectations.

reflection: |
  Today was a good day because I reached an important milestone by completing all of my research experiments. The process took longer than I originally expected, but it required me to become more careful with data preparation, variable management, model evaluation, and debugging. I learned that research code often requires repeated checking because a small issue with labels, indices, or data alignment can affect the validity of an entire experiment.
  I also appreciated the opportunity to learn about brain-computer interfaces from Dr. Crawford. His work demonstrated how interdisciplinary research can bring together computer science, engineering, neuroscience, and medicine. It encouraged me to think beyond the immediate goals of my project and consider how methods for interpreting physiological signals can contribute to a wide range of healthcare and human-computer interaction applications.
  Watching the cohort's pitch videos was another highlight of the day. I admired the amount of creativity each group brought to presenting its project. The videos showed that effective research communication does not always have to rely on long technical explanations. A short, well-planned presentation can give an audience a meaningful understanding of a complex project. Seeing everyone's work also reminded me of how much progress the entire cohort has made throughout the summer.
  Beginning the documentation process after lunch helped me transition from experimentation to interpretation and communication. Now that the experiments are complete, my focus will shift toward reviewing the outputs with my teammates, identifying the strongest and most defensible findings, and presenting both the successful and unexpected results clearly. I feel relieved and proud to have completed this stage of the project, and I am looking forward to seeing how the final report and presentation come together.
