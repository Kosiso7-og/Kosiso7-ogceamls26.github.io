what_i_learned: |
  Today began in the research lab, where I continued working on my experiments involving ECG and PPG biosignals. I focused primarily on reviewing the results from Experiment 2 and Experiment 3 and trying to better understand how the performance of the individual drift classifiers influenced the outcomes of the CFD reliability comparison. Experiment 3 compares the CFD-optimal feature pair, a non-CFD redundant pair, and a model that uses all six ECG and PPG domains. Although I expected the CFD-optimal architecture to achieve the highest Drift Classification Accuracy, the three models produced very similar results.
  This encouraged me to look more closely at the foundation of the experiment. I reviewed the ECG and PPG classifier results from Experiment 2, including DCA, AUC, Energy-Drift Correlation, Stability Violation Rate, and the ECG–PPG agreement rate. I learned that strong EDC values and low SVR values do not automatically mean that a classifier is accurately distinguishing benign from non-benign perturbations. A model can respond consistently to changes in its latent representation while still producing predictions that are close to chance. This helped me understand why it is important to evaluate reliability using several metrics rather than relying on only one result.
  After lunch, we attended a SAIRI Connect session where we discussed the field day scheduled for next week. The session provided information about the event and gave us an opportunity to prepare for the activities and expectations surrounding it. After the session, I returned to my research and continued examining my experimental pipeline, particularly how the clean signals, perturbed signals, labels, and model inputs were created.

blockers: |
  My main blocker today was that the results from Experiment 3 did not follow the expected pattern. The CFD-optimal model, non-CFD model, and all-six-domain model produced very similar Drift Classification Accuracy scores, and the calculated Complementarity Reliability Gain was negative and not statistically significant. This meant that the experiment did not currently support the hypothesis that CFD-optimal domain selection improves overall drift classification accuracy.
  While reviewing the earlier code, I also noticed that some of the Experiment 2 models may have been trained using randomly assigned benign and non-benign labels without directly providing the models with corresponding clean and perturbed signal pairs. If the labels are not connected to meaningful differences in the signal data, the models would have little opportunity to learn the distinction between benign and non-benign drift. This may help explain why the ECG and PPG classifiers performed close to chance and why their predicted probabilities remained close to 0.50.
  I also feel slightly behind where I would like to be in the research timeline. The experiments require careful validation, and correcting one part of the pipeline may require me to rerun later experiments. To address this, I need to dedicate additional time outside the regular lab sessions to review the code, correct the experimental design where necessary, and document each step more carefully.

reflection: |
  Today reminded me that unexpected research results are not necessarily failures. When an experiment does not produce the expected outcome, it creates an opportunity to examine the methodology more carefully and identify assumptions that may have influenced the result. Although it was initially discouraging to see that the CFD-optimal model did not outperform the other architectures, reviewing the full pipeline helped me better understand the relationship between data preparation, perturbation labels, latent-space drift, and final classification performance.
  I also learned that metrics must be interpreted together. The high ECG–PPG agreement rate in Experiment 2 initially appeared promising, but the low classification accuracy showed that two models can frequently agree while still making incorrect decisions. Similarly, the strong EDC values indicated that embedding changes were related to output changes, but they did not prove that the models could correctly identify whether the changes were benign or non-benign. These distinctions are helping me become more critical and intentional when interpreting machine-learning results.
  The SAIRI Connect session was also a helpful break from the technical work and gave us a chance to discuss the upcoming field day. I am looking forward to participating next week and spending time with the other students in the program.
  Although I feel somewhat behind, I know that rushing through the remaining experiments would create more problems later. My goal is to dedicate extra time to the project, correct the Experiment 2 pipeline where necessary, and make sure the results I report are methodologically defensible. Taking the time to understand and fix the underlying issues will ultimately strengthen both my research and my final presentation.
