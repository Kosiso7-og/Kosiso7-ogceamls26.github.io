what_i_learned: |
  Today began with our research lab session, where our graduate mentor asked us to run the experimental pipeline on the BIDMC dataset.I worked through the experiments again, including feature-domain extraction, complementary domain discovery, drift evaluation, multimodal comparison, and reliability analysis. This time, I added more safeguards throughout the code. I included checks to confirm that the ECG, PPG, and respiratory arrays contained the same number of samples, that the labels matched the feature matrices, and that the training and testing indices stayed within the correct range. I also added clearer error messages so that problems with missing variables, incorrect shapes, or misaligned data could be identified before model training began.
  Another improvement was the addition of more visualizations. Rather than relying only on numerical outputs, I created figures that made it easier to compare the models, trust levels, drift behavior, and performance under different experimental conditions. These visualizations helped turn long tables of metrics into results that could be interpreted more quickly. They will also be useful when we begin preparing our final presentation because they communicate the patterns in the data more clearly than raw numbers alone.
  While working with the BIDMC data, I also had to recreate some of the ECG and PPG feature-domain dictionaries after realizing that the extracted variables were no longer available in memory. The original signals were still present, so I re-extracted the time, frequency, and time-frequency representations for all 3,515 samples. This reminded me that notebook environments are temporary and that important intermediate outputs should be saved whenever possible instead of relying entirely on the current runtime.

blockers: |
  The main challenge today was repeating a large experimental workflow while making sure that the BIDMC data remained separate from the MIMIC data used in the earlier experiments. Because both datasets contain similar physiological signals, it would have been easy to reuse the wrong labels, indices, or variables accidentally. I had to carefully confirm the size and identity of each array before continuing.
  I also encountered situations where some extracted-domain variables were no longer in memory. The ECG, PPG, and respiratory signals were available, but the dictionaries containing the frequency and time-frequency features needed to be recreated. Although this was not a major issue, it slowed the workflow and reinforced the importance of saving intermediate results.
  Another challenge was ensuring that the additional safeguards did not simply allow the code to run, but actually protected the validity of the experiments. A model can still produce an output even when labels and features are misaligned, so I added validation checks before the training and evaluation stages. This required more time, but it reduced the risk of interpreting results generated from inconsistent data.

reflection: |
  Today felt slower than many of the previous research days. Part of that may be because we are approaching the end of the program and much of the work now involves repeating, checking, documenting, and refining tasks rather than starting something completely new. The excitement of building the initial pipeline has gradually shifted toward the more detailed process of validating the results and making sure the work is ready to present.
  Even though the day felt slow, it was still productive. Re-running the experiments on the BIDMC dataset helped me see the difference between writing code that works once and developing a pipeline that can be applied reliably to multiple datasets. The additional checks and visualizations made the workflow more organized and reduced some of the uncertainty I experienced while working through the earlier experiments.
  I also realized that repetition is an important part of research. Running the same experimental design on another dataset can reveal whether a result is consistent or whether it depends heavily on the original data. Even when the outputs are not identical, the comparison can help explain the strengths and limitations of the method.
